What HDR Is Actually For—and Why Godzilla Minus One Gets It Right

Shrey Tyagi • 26 Nov, 2026

There is a widespread misconception about what High Dynamic Range (HDR) is supposed to do. Many people assume HDR exists to make everything on screen look blindingly bright, to turn every image into sparkling glitter, or to artificially crank up the contrast.

It doesn't.

Yes, when you take advantage of HDR's brighter luminance range, the overall perception of contrast naturally increases as a byproduct. But increasing contrast is not the objective of HDR—if artificially boosting contrast were the goal, you could just turn up a setting on any TV.

(A quick note on scope: HDR is often paired with Wide Color Gamut formats like Rec. 2020 or the DCI-P3 gamut used in cinemas, which expand color alongside brightness. For this piece, however, I'm setting color aside to focus purely on HDR itself—luminance and dynamic range.)

At its core, HDR exists for one fundamental reason: to solve the limitations of SDR.

The SDR Compromise

In Standard Dynamic Range (SDR)—which is built around a 100-nit mastering standard—filmmakers and colorists are constantly forced into an uncomfortable trade-off.

When grading a shot in SDR, the top priority is making the principal subject look right: natural skin tones, balanced brightness, and pleasing contrast. The problem is that getting the main subject right almost always forces you to sacrifice the highlights.

Try to protect those bright highlights instead, and you end up dragging down the exposure of the entire scene, sabotaging the lighting on your main characters and making them far too dark. Try to squeeze both the shadows and the bright highlights into SDR's narrow container at the same time, and you flatten the image out—leaving it looking dull, washed-out, and unrealistic.

Now, blowing out the highlights can occasionally be a deliberate artistic choice. A filmmaker might intentionally push highlights to pure white to emulate a vintage filmic look, or to craft a distinct visual signature like Steven Spielberg did in his 2026 alien film, Disclosure Day.

Most of the time, however, directors aren't actively trying to clip their highlights. It simply happens as a necessary compromise: they have to protect the look of the main subject, and losing the highlights is the price SDR makes them pay.

What HDR Actually Fixes

This is where HDR steps in. Modern TVs are capable of reaching remarkably high brightness levels, but that doesn't mean filmmakers should make the entire frame bright. Doing so would be uncomfortable on the viewer's eyes and would completely undermine the mood and artistic intent of a scene.

Instead, HDR lets a colorist keep the main subject at the exact same comfortable, natural brightness you would expect, while using the extra headroom of the HDR container exclusively for highlights, light sources, and bright specular elements that couldn't fit inside SDR's 100-nit ceiling. Rather than clipping out into blank white, all the extra luminance information the camera originally captured gets to stay intact.

To picture how this works in practice, consider three everyday cinematic scenarios:

  • The Dark-Room Conversation: Imagine a scene inside a dimly lit room where several characters are talking. The colorist grades the shot carefully so that the lighting, colors, and contrast look optimal and every character's face is clearly visible. In SDR, doing this pushes the practical light bulbs, the table lamp, and the bright daylight spilling in from outside the window straight into blown-out white. In HDR, all of that highlight detail is recovered—the faces stay properly exposed, the contrast and mood remain untouched, and the light sources still hold the information the camera captured.
  • The Shaded Evening Exterior: Now imagine a scene set in a shaded area of a slum during the evening. Even though the evening sky isn't midday-bright, the shaded area itself is quite dark. Once the colorist grades the scene so that the houses and the people's faces are clearly visible, the brighter sky above gets completely blown away in SDR. With HDR, the foreground stays properly lit and natural, yet you can still look up and see the texture and detail of the clouds in the sky—without making the scene look dark or flat.
  • The Sudden Explosion: Finally, imagine an evenly lit scene interrupted by an intense, blindingly bright explosion. In SDR, you're stuck with two bad options: either those searing flames clip into flat, featureless white, or you flatten the entire frame to accommodate them, making the scene look unnatural. With HDR, the extra dynamic range is reserved specifically for the intense brightness of the flames, while the rest of the frame remains an evenly, realistically graded scene.

When HDR is applied like this—quietly solving exposure compromises rather than screaming for attention—it is being used at its absolute best.

Reference-Level HDR in Action: Godzilla Minus One

I was genuinely thrilled to see this exact, textbook approach to HDR executed to perfection in a movie I recently watched: Godzilla Minus One.

Watching it in Dolby Vision on my Sony Bravia TV, it immediately stood out that the entire film was graded using the exact philosophy described above. In fact, all three scenarios I just walked through—the dim interior conversation, the shaded evening slum beneath a cloudy sky, and the blazing explosions—are actual scenes straight out of the movie.

If you want to see how HDR is truly supposed to be done, Godzilla Minus One is the movie to watch. It won't assault your eyes with glittery, hyper-contrasty demo-reel visuals. Instead, it delivers the ideal, restrained implementation of HDR—exactly the way the format was theoretically envisioned to work.
